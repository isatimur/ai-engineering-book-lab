# Shipping something to someone always wins — Kenneth Auchenberg (ex. Stripe, VSCode)

## Summary
Kenneth Auchenberg was on the early VS Code team at Microsoft, then built a large part of Stripe's developer platform, and is now a partner at an early-stage venture fund in New York. His one product principle is the title: shipping something to someone always wins. Products succeed through the number of iterations you crank on a problem, not the number of big launches at big conferences.

**Skateboard, not wheels**
The tired way to build a car is wheels, then chassis, then engine; nothing is usable until the end, so there is no feedback. The wired way is skateboard, scooter, bike, then car: each stage moves a person from A to B, and every change reaches real users. A continuously viable product is, in his experience, many orders of magnitude more valuable than one that is only viable at the end.

**The one-day loop**
At Stripe, before any major or trap-door design decision, a new project needed a feedback loop: real users who could see something, a way to hear from them, and the ability to ship an improvement — in less than a day, ideally hours. If you cannot run the loop in a day, "your process is broken." You need not ship daily, but you must be able to.

**Tactics from Stripe and VS Code**
- Build for real people with a name and an email you can call, not a research persona, and learn how they solve the problem today.
- Write the PR-FAQ or launch blog post first. It forces specificity and a clear position. For APIs, draft the API outline and show it to users before any prototype.
- Design the best product before you weigh legal, compliance and financial limits. Counterparts explain risk; the product owner makes the call. By launch, it must tick every box.
- Prototype: with AI coding tools there is no excuse, technical or not.
- Seek high-bandwidth feedback over dashboards: shadow customers as they integrate, share a Slack or Discord channel, text with users, watch API responses for where people get stuck. Early on it should feel like a professional services firm serving five people. Most products fail not because few people use them but because they are useful to no one. At VS Code he was in users' DMs, working out why CSS completions or the JavaScript debugger failed.
- APIs are much harder than UI. A change that takes you an afternoon can mean a six-month migration for a customer, so iterate with a small, trusted group of discerning developers.

**What AI changes**
Design, product and engineering roles are merging, and he uses AI tools for prototyping, coding, customer research and meeting notes. His hot take: nothing changes in the craft. AI speeds up every step, but the job is the same. As the cost of code goes to zero, product management matters more, because knowing who you build for becomes the constraint. In an essay on what he calls AI-native founders, the winners build tastefully, understand customers deeply, keep high iteration velocity, and can distribute and sell — "a lot like product management."

He closes with a tweet from someone at OpenAI: shipping is a low-entropy state and "the universe does not want you to ship," so do it anyway. When a product debate stalls, ship something to someone and let real feedback settle it.

## Why it matters
- Sets a measurable bar for iteration: a user-to-improvement loop in under a day, or the process is broken.
- The skateboard metaphor gives a clear test for any AI feature plan: is each stage usable by a real person?
- Writing the launch post or PR-FAQ before building is a cheap, concrete way to force specificity, now even faster with AI drafting.
- The point that APIs are far harder to change than UI is a direct warning for teams shipping agent-facing APIs and data structures.
- Argues that as code gets cheap, customer knowledge and taste become the scarce skills, which shapes how AI-era product teams should hire and organise.
