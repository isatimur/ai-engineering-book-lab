# AI Engineers: The Next Generation — Stefania Druga, Google Gemini

## Summary
Stefania Druga wants to open AI engineering to the next generation: young people, much earlier. Her case: 70% of AI users are Gen Z, students already use generative AI for homework, and many prefer it to human tutors. She wants kids to be the engineers and designers of these tools, not only their users.

**Teaching AI through Scratch**
Scratch is a free, open-source visual programming platform from MIT with over 100 million child users. In 2015, during her master's in that lab, Druga began an extension that lets children train their own models, build games and program hardware. Kids trained image and text classifiers, used off-the-shelf sentiment analysis and image classification, and reprogrammed voice assistants to remember their preferences. One child trained a unicorns-versus-narwhals model and saw from the low confidence score that he needed more hand-drawn examples, because most of his images were cartoons. Druga says this builds data literacy. Tested in public and private schools and community centers, she found that "kids are actually like little scientists": they form hypotheses about the model, test them and refine them. Her study showed a significant change in how much intelligence children attributed to smart devices after they trained and programmed models. The platform is translated into 50 languages.

**Mock the copilot before you build it**
During the pandemic she ran design studies with families in 10 US states. There was no working assistant: "I was the AI," in a Wizard of Oz study over chat. Kids did not want the copilot to do everything. They wanted coding ideas, help to elaborate them, and encouragement. One 12-year-old said the hardest part is when you run into a wall and are out of ideas. Children spent double their usual time programming. Parents were still needed for distraction, sibling disputes and hard concepts such as clones and broadcast. She then built an evaluation benchmark of over 100 Scratch programs and ran it against state-of-the-art models on explaining code, building learning exercises, debugging and generating ideas.

**The copilot study**
The new copilot is a code editor plus an AI chat that calls a web server. She evaluates several models, including one fine-tuned on Scratch projects, and it generates art assets. She tested it with 18 young people from 11 countries in their own languages. It gave conceptual, design, navigation and encouragement support. A student from Jamaica went from zero to a working program. Kids also asked for character names and plots, and often refused suggestions; when one did, the copilot replied "no worries, it's your game, have fun."

**Design lessons**
- Prioritise agency: by default the copilot asks questions and gives a hint only when the student is really stuck, after the same question is asked three times.
- Use the agent as a starting point against the "cold start" problem.
- Allow customization: voice or typing, three ideas or one.
- Kids want the agent to move blocks with them, to watch simulations of several agents building a game, and to get assets straight on the stage.
- When it cannot do something, it should say why and state its limits.

She closes with a live demo and a link to the EU AI Act, which asks providers of AI systems to ensure sufficient AI literacy among staff and users.

## Why it matters
- A concrete case for mocking an AI assistant with a human (Wizard of Oz) before building it, to learn what users actually want from it.
- The hint-after-three-questions default is a specific, testable rule for balancing help and challenge in a tutoring or coding agent.
- Kids asked for ideas, not finished code, and often turned down suggestions. A copilot that keeps the user in charge can raise engagement, here to double the usual programming time.
- The benchmark of over 100 Scratch programs shows how to evaluate models on a narrow domain before you ship a copilot in it.
- The EU AI Act literacy duty gives teams a regulatory reason to make their agents explain their own limits.
