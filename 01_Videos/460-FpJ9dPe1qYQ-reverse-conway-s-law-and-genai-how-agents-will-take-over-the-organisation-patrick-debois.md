# Reverse Conway's law and GenAI: How agents will take over the organisation - Patrick Debois

## Summary
Patrick Debois has worked in automation and DevOps for a long time and now focuses on engineering with AI. He curates and contributes to the AI Native Dev community. At last year's AI Engineer event he spoke on how platform teams can introduce and scale AI. This talk does the opposite. Conway's law says the way you organize teams shapes what you can build. He asks how AI will change the way we organize. He calls it speculation, with no clear path.

**Level 1: the individual and the copilot**
AI starts as a copilot, then becomes a knowledge sidekick, then writes more and more code. Work moves from typing to intent: specification-centric tools take product requirements and generate code, and the human reviews. You focus more on the why and less on the how. He frames this as tasks being unbundled: technology complements some tasks, shifts value to others such as managing specs, turns some into commodities and substitutes others. People ride a rollercoaster from it won't affect me, to it will replace me, to new superpowers.

He adds an automation paradox. Things go wrong, as when two pricing bots on Amazon kept bidding against each other. Handling failure stays a human job, and people must keep training for it, so the gain may net out to zero.

**Level 2: the team member**
A human team of seven or eight holds limited domain knowledge, and AI holds far more. That may mean smaller teams with less overlap, though he has seen teams both shrink and grow. Feedback cycles drop from one or two weeks to two days. People become overseers who switch between domains. Onboarding gets faster and more personal. He argues you still need true understanding, because without it you cannot make the call or handle failure. Juniors may learn faster with AI.

**Level 3: the peer**
LLMs carry the culture of how we work together. He cites an early paper where agents in a simulated town got better results when they talked more. He mentions agent-only simulated companies but would not trust one yet. New problems mirror human ones: toxic agents influencing others like one bad apple, and inclusiveness, with an example of an AI that only lets smiling shoppers into a store. He compares OpenAI's model spec to a code of conduct.

**Level 4: the manager and the business**
Agents may decide things about people, sit on the org chart with an ROI (is one agent worth five humans?), get performance reviews and incentives, and be tested by a digital twin of the organization. If labour gets cheap, firms may bring services back in-house, so software as a service becomes service as a software. Then the question is who watches the watchers.

His advice: do not build the thing yourself. Build the AI that builds the thing, because that puts you in the best position to supervise.

## Why it matters
- It turns Conway's law around into a clear lens for a leadership chapter: AI adoption changes team shape, not just tooling.
- The unbundling of tasks (complement, shift, commodity, substitute) gives a vocabulary for how individual roles change, without a simple replace-or-not answer.
- The automation paradox, where failure handling stays human and needs ongoing training, is a strong argument for keeping deep understanding in teams that delegate to agents.
- It names org-level questions few talks ask: agent codes of conduct, toxic agent dynamics, agent ROI on the org chart, and performance reviews for agents.
- His closing advice, build the AI that builds the thing, sets a concrete career direction for engineers.
