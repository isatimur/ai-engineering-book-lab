# AI Engineering Without Borders — swyx

## Summary
swyx gives this ten-minute address remotely ("I wish I was there in person") to the AI Engineer World's Fair, on the one-year anniversary of the Rise of the AI Engineer document. He opens on borders: an innately human, made-up constraint he faces personally. AI, by contrast, "is a border disrespect". An LLM trained mostly on English learns other languages as a side effect; you can turn Llama into a vision-language model with about 100 dollars of post-training; it does not separate hallucination from memorized world knowledge; and it does not respect copyright.

**Definitions are not enough**
He lists the definitions he has helped spread: the API line between AI engineers and ML people, the three types from the last summit (AI-enhanced engineer, AI products engineer, non-human agentic AI engineer), and job-role lists such as one discussed with Elicit on his podcast. Each is workable, but agreeing to disagree is a cop-out, because there is no shared ground truth.

**The tracks are arbitrary, and he made them up**
The conference is his highest-stakes statement of the state of AI engineering. Last year's tracks (RAG, code gen, agents, multimodality) return, and as the field matures the concerns multiply: migrating to open models, building evals ("maybe I should have done that first"), scaling inference, deploying to the Fortune 500, hiring teams and setting AI strategy. The most important track is the community, which cannot be sold on a website. But the tracks came from a list of disciplines in his original document. Why separate agents from code gen, or RAG from open models? Like company org charts, these borders are made up; another group would draw different ones.

**Toward laws of AI engineering**
IEEE and Google define software engineering as design, implementation, testing, documentation and life-cycle management. Definitions of real engineering add applying natural sciences for the benefit of humanity, which software engineering lacks. AI engineering sits between: software engineering that meets more real-world constraints. He proposes laws that, like gravity, any observer would rediscover:
- Constants: humans speak at about 80 words per minute but read at 200.
- Contingent facts: once Apple Intelligence ships a local model on every phone, its advertised 30 tokens per second becomes the baseline for intelligence too cheap to meter.
- Trends, the Moore's laws of AI, worth planning around so you avoid bad bets. Context: a year ago a Mosaic 7B model offered a lossy 60,000 to 70,000 tokens; now people in the room have trained million-token windows, and Anthropic's latest model improves context utilization, not just length. Cost: a 99.55% decline in the cost of GPT-3-level intelligence in two years, and perhaps 80 to 90% for GPT-4-level as newer models such as Llama 3 arrive.

**Where AI engineers stand**
One axis of safety vs acceleration cannot place AI engineering. He adds a second axis, utility: "we are utility Maxis above all else". His closing ask: disrespect borders, avoid dogma and lazy consensus, disagree productively with yourself, each other and the status quo, and jump between tracks, friend groups, disciplines and modalities.

## Why it matters
- A founding voice of the term admits that the role definitions and conference tracks are his own arbitrary borders, which is useful context when any chapter leans on them.
- The constants, contingent facts and trends framework gives a method for separating durable design assumptions from temporary ones.
- Concrete trend numbers (99.55% cost decline for GPT-3-level, context growing from about 70,000 lossy tokens to a million) support planning-for-trends arguments.
- The 80 vs 200 words-per-minute speak-read gap is a crisp design constant for voice and chat interfaces.
- The utility-maximizer framing sets AI engineering apart from the safety-vs-acceleration debate.
