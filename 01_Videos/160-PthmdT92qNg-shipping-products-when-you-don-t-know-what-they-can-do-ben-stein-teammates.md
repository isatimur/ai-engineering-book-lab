# Shipping Products When You Don't Know What they Can Do — Ben Stein, Teammates

## Summary
Ben Stein, co-founder of Teammates (a platform for designing AI "digital workforce" personas like Stacy, an L3 engineer with her own Slack and Gmail identity), opens with a customer asking whether they could tag their teammate in a Google Doc comment — a question he realized he genuinely could not answer, because the product is built on LLMs whose capabilities are unknown even to its makers, layered under an open-ended free-text interface. He argues this forces product management to change in three ways: designing for "affordances" (what an agent can do) rather than specific feature requirements; treating capability as emergent, discovered after building rather than specified up front; and using evals as the actual product spec (e.g., "should be snarky but not mean 80% of the time"), which is why he wants product people, not just engineers, watching eval results. He also describes vibe-coding prototypes as the only way to "feel" an agent interaction before committing to it (citing users hating a version of the agent that asked too many clarifying questions), notes that bug triage becomes ambiguous when there's no spec to violate, and describes shifting customer conversations away from "visionary" or "honest broker" framing toward "we're inventing this together," since neither the future nor the present behavior of the product can be confidently described.

## Why it matters
- Gives a first-person account of how product management practice is being rewritten for agentic products — affordances over feature specs, evals functioning as the spec, emergent behavior discovery — grounded in a concrete anecdote (Google Doc comment tagging) rather than abstraction.
- Useful evidence for a chapter on shipping under genuine capability uncertainty: even a founder building the product cannot predict what an LLM-based agent will do, which reframes bug triage, QA, and customer expectation-setting.
